⚖️ Important Behavioural Correlation Disclaimer

This system calculates estimates of observable study behaviours (head pose, eye openness, phone presence, absence), which may correlate with attention but do not directly measure mental concentration.

  • Looking away does not prove distraction (the student may be reading physical notes or contemplating).
  • Closing eyes does not prove sleep or fatigue (the student may be reflecting or resting eyes).
  • Absence from view indicates leaving the camera frame, not stopping learning.
  • Phone presence indicates object visibility in view, not necessarily active smartphone use.
  • Looking towards the screen does not prove mental concentration.

Engineering Principles: Observable Behaviour vs Mental Focus

The Synspeak Monitoring System is designed in strict accordance with the scientific principle that computer vision systems can only measure physical and observable behaviours, not internal cognitive mental states.

1. Looking Away vs Cognitive Distraction

Looking away from a screen is common when solving problems on scrap paper, reading a textbook, or reflecting. The system flags this as "Head Turned Away", an observable behaviour, rather than an objective mental distraction.

2. Eye Closure vs Sleep

Brief eye closures are normal blinks (100–300ms). Sustained eye closure may reflect contemplation or eye strain, not necessarily sleep. Our system uses a duration filter to avoid misclassifying natural blinking.

3. Phone Detection vs Phone Usage

A phone lying on the desk may be detected by YOLO object detection even if the student is ignoring it. The metric records "Phone Visible in Camera Frame", not proven usage.

4. Desk Absence vs Study Cessation

Stepping away from the camera could mean taking a planned break, retrieving a textbook, or stretching. The system records "Desk Absence Duration".

5. On-Screen Gaze vs Mental Concentration

Looking directly at the monitor does not prove that cognitive focus is high (a student can daydream while staring at the screen). Hence, the concentration index is explicitly defined as an estimated behavioural engagement proxy.
